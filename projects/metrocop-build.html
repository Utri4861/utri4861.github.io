I started this build in 2019. This journal follows the work from the beginning: early foam experiments, helmet rebuilds, convention deadlines, and the refinements that came after.

Follow the experiments year by year, from the first foam panels to finished costumes and redesigned accessories. The optional “From the community” sections collect feedback, questions, and other builders’ contributions along the way.

About this journal & design credits

Adapted from my original build notes, with dates retained to show how the project developed. Some early entries include later photographs. Community contributions are summarized and attributed, with links to the original discussions. Historical prices and resource availability may have changed.

Reference images are retained beside the discussion they illustrate. Design references include mb814’s model and textures, Half-Life: Alyx references and Max Aristov, and imagery from msleeper’s blog. The early baton uses Lybos’s model; the later stunstick references Pixelitz’s HD remake. The 2021 helmet came from an Etsy model whose creator is not identified in my original notes. Half-Life and its characters belong to Valve; this is an independent fan project.

The revised vest template linked in the 2025 update supersedes the older template links and preview image.

2019

The first prototypes

January 27, 2019 · Erik Reiner

A dream build begins

I've wanted to build a Metrocop for years. Seeing other makers bring Civil Protection to life finally pushed me to start my own version.

The goal is to get as close as I can to the character's proportions and silhouette. My main reference is mb814's higher-resolution Metrocop model and textures: https://gamebanana.com/skins/141967. I prefer its look to the original in several places, although I'm planning to skip the lace-up boots.

Looking ahead: these later photographs show how far the costume developed from this starting point.

January 28, 2019 · Erik Reiner

The first helmet

The helmet is my starting point. I split the reference model into two pieces to fit my Ender 3 and began testing the shape in print.

The first results made the limitations pretty obvious. A smaller bust helped me see the whole model, but the faceting was still too pronounced for the finish I wanted.

At this point, SketchUp was the only modeling software I knew. After an unsuccessful attempt to smooth the mesh in Windows 3D Builder, I paid an artist on Fiverr $17 to do it. That made a substantial difference.

This print is still a prototype: I made it too narrow to wear. One possibility is to turn it into a vacuum-forming buck, but getting the shape right is the first hurdle.

January 29, 2019 · Erik Reiner

Building the vest

Sketching the vest helped me work out its construction. I don't need to copy how the game model is assembled; I need to reproduce how it looks.

My approach is to make each raised panel from foam, wrap the panels individually, and connect them from behind. That should give the vest the definition of armor rather than the softness of a quilted jacket.

I started with the center panels.

Short strips of cloth connect the foam cores along the back.

The first mock-up was too flat. The basic method seems promising, but the panels need more shaping before the silhouette works.

January 30, 2019 · Erik Reiner

Choosing the construction method

I'm deliberately avoiding sewing through a thick layer of batting for the vest. It isn't about avoiding the sewing machine; that construction simply wouldn't give me the defined panels I'm after.

Separate foam cores should let me control the shape of each section while keeping the connections flexible.

January 30, 2019 · Erik Reiner

Foam panels and cloth hinges

I set the fabric aside and focused on completing the foam panels. The covering can come later; first I need a structure that fits together.

I glued strips of cloth across the seams on the back, connecting the panels without filling the gaps between them.

The vest is starting to take shape. There are a surprising number of separate pieces involved.

January 31, 2019 · Erik Reiner

A collar, a test fit, and upholstery foam

With more panels connected, the vest is finally becoming a three-dimensional object.

I eyeballed the collar, and it still needs work. The reference flares outward more than mine does.

A quick test fit was encouraging.

Then I took it apart again. Photographing each panel on the cutting mat gave me a record I could eventually turn into templates.

For the next experiment, I salvaged upholstery foam from a discarded chair and used it to pad the panels before wrapping them. This is much closer to the surface I had in mind.

February 1, 2019 · Erik Reiner

Wrapping the vest

The first fabric covering is taking shape. This is spare cloth for testing the method, not the final material.

The collar was tricky to wrap, but it came together reasonably well.

The fit was a little lucky. Rather than measuring everything against myself, I scaled the panels by eye, comparing the width of the reference's center chest panel with the helmet I'd already printed.

Next are the underjacket, trousers, boots, and printed accessories. I'm considering adding a white stripe to a pair of green pants I already have, but the overall proportions will decide whether they stay.

2020

References, resources & a restart

May 6, 2020 · Erik Reiner

Test fits and Half-Life: Alyx references

I put together a rough outfit from clothes I had available to test the overall silhouette. The new jackboots are a little chunkier than I'd like, and these trousers are only placeholders. I'm aiming for flat-fronted pants in cotton or twill.

Half-Life: Alyx has given me another set of references to consider. Its Civil Protection design keeps the familiar outline but changes several details.

The vest appears smaller, the boots fit more closely, and the jacket looks more like something I could adapt from an existing garment. This older comparison image, sourced from msleeper's blog, helps show the difference.

The white shoulders and chest reflector still need careful planning. I'm leaning toward a combination of the original design, mb814's interpretation, and selected details from Alyx, while keeping the finished costume recognizably Half-Life 2.

May 6, 2020 · Erik Reiner

Comparing the new Metrocop design

I've been studying Max Aristov's Civil Protection designs for Half-Life: Alyx: https://www.artstation.com/maxaristov/prints.

The biggest change is the vest. It's more streamlined, with broad straps in place of some rib and shoulder panels. The gloves have buckles and white stripes, while the boots have a closer fit, inside zippers, and white strips on the outer legs.

The mask and overall silhouette remain familiar. I like that continuity, although I don't plan to reproduce the chest cutout in the newer vest. For this costume, the older chest layout still feels like the right choice.

May 7, 2020 · Erik Reiner

Searching for the jacket

Finding the right jacket is a balance of material, length, pocket placement, and price. Some soft-shell options have the right surface but are too short in the body.

A longer field-style jacket with front pockets looks more promising.

One candidate was around $30, leaving some room in the budget for tailoring. It would need the zipper flap removed and possibly a rear center vent. At 6'1", I need enough length to keep the costume's proportions convincing.

None of these is an exact match. The pockets, shoulders, and torso length all involve compromises, so I'm checking the whole silhouette before committing.

May 12, 2020 · Erik Reiner

The original budget

Historical estimates from 2020, not current prices or a final project total.

I worked through an early budget to see where the money would go. The foam vest was inexpensive: an $8 pack of floor mats and fabric from my grandma. The first mask prototype added $17 for mesh work and roughly $6 in printing plastic.

My other estimates included $56.67 for boots, $32 each for gloves and a jacket, $20 for trousers, $8 for belts, $6 for printed belt boxes, and $8 for their electronics. Ribbon was another small expense.

These were planning figures, not a final cost report. Buying and adapting existing clothing looked more practical than making every garment from scratch. I could keep prototyping the helmet and accessories while spacing out the larger purchases.

Helmet assembly & test fits

January 22, 2022 · Erik Reiner

Magnets, paint, and assembly

The helmet is painted and assembled. I used masking tape to hold the outer pieces together while positioning the magnets inside.

The tape also kept the mating magnets separated during fitting.

With the finish applied, the individual pieces finally read as one helmet.

A test fit with the vest shows how the two largest components work together.

There is still plenty to finish: more detail on the belt boxes, paint on the stunstick, and white shoulder sections for the jacket. I'm considering padded shoulder pieces attached with hook-and-loop fasteners.

The trousers need their white stripes, and I'm still working on the drape around the boots. The motorcycle gloves are ready; the lower half of the costume remains the biggest fit challenge.

December 25, 2022 · Erik Reiner

Jacket, gloves, and boots

For the clothing, shape matters more than finding one exact product listing. The jacket needed front pockets and enough length; the gloves needed a slim gauntlet profile.

Women's motorcycle gauntlets offered a more streamlined shape than many men's versions, although sizing required care. The boots I used were Harley Davidson boots bought secondhand. Round-toe riding, engineer, or harness styles can offer a similar outline, depending on the details.

January 11, 2023 · Erik Reiner

How the vest was made

The prototype vest came together in roughly a day or two of hands-on work. Sketching it from several angles gave me a mental model, and I used the center chest square as a reference for the surrounding pieces.

I cut the foam panels, joined their backs with cloth strips, and tried the assembly on. Where it didn't fit, I trimmed; where it did, I moved on to heat-forming.

Once the shape worked, I took it apart, glued batting to the front of each panel, wrapped the panels in fabric, and reconnected them from behind. The pattern was largely developed by eye, so later revisions still had room to improve the proportions.

December 3, 2025 · Erik Reiner

Designing for assembly

The “filters” below are ventilated costume parts, not protective respirators.

I'm revisiting the component models in Fusion 360, with more attention to how the printed parts will actually go together.

The belt boxes are designed around functionality as well as appearance. Watching Will Cogley's work got me thinking more carefully about assembly, print orientation, and hiding visible marks from the printing process.

I also developed ventilated costume filter components to make the mask less stuffy on the convention floor. These are costume parts, not protective respirators.

The updated stunstick is based on Pixelitz's Half-Life 2 HD remake. I'm especially pleased with how it assembles and takes paint.

These versions were printed on my Bambu P1S. The Ender 3 that started the project is still around, but the newer printer has become my main machine for this work.
