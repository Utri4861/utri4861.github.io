A costume build that became a long-running design project.

3D Printing Sewing Upholstery Fusion 360 Electronics Airbrush

I started this project in 2019 with a simple goal: make the Metrocop costume I’d always wanted. I wanted to capture the proportions, silhouette, and unsettling familiarity of Half-Life 2’s Civil Protection officers—and make something I could actually wear.

That first costume took roughly four years to bring together. Along the way, it became my introduction to new tools, materials, and ways of building. Even after taking it to WonderCon in 2023, I kept coming back to it.

Finding the shape

Getting the look right meant paying attention to how everything worked together. The helmet, segmented vest, jacket, and belt equipment all needed to feel like parts of the same uniform.

The helmet went through early prints, fit problems, and a restart using a different model. For the vest, I experimented with foam panels, fabric coverings, and flexible connections, trying to create something that looked armored without becoming a rigid shell—or a quilted jacket.

A lot of the work was straightforward trial and error: assemble it, try it on, compare it to references, and take it apart again.

Learning through the build

The costume brought together 3D printing, foam fabrication, garment modifications, painting, and electronics. Some parts began with existing models and sourced clothing; others I developed myself as my skills grew.

The voice system became my first Arduino project, using a Nootropic Design Audio Hacker shield. Designing its enclosure was my first project in Fusion 360. Those small additions opened up entirely new parts of the hobby for me.

By March 2023, the costume was ready for WonderCon. Wearing it was a milestone, but it wasn’t really the end of the project.

Built, worn, rebuilt

Later versions became an opportunity to rethink the details—not just how they looked, but how they printed, assembled, and felt to wear.

I revisited the belt boxes, developed ventilated costume filter components, and worked on an updated stunstick based on Pixelitz’s Half-Life 2 HD remake. As I spent more time in Fusion 360, I became more deliberate about part breakdowns, assembly, and hiding the marks left by printing.

Building additional Metrocop costumes gave me more opportunities to refine those choices. What started as one personal build gradually became a repeatable process, while still leaving plenty to improve.

Sharing the process

I documented the project on The RPF from the beginning, including the experiments and revisions along the way. I also shared vest templates and answered questions about materials, construction, and sourcing.

One of the most rewarding parts has been seeing other people use that information for their own builds. The thread became more than a record of my costume—it became a place for other builders to contribute ideas, share results, and help the next person get started.

From personal project to Citadel Supply Co.

That ongoing work eventually grew into Citadel Supply Co., where I’m focusing on props and accessories informed by the same process: study the reference, build it, test it, and keep refining it.

The Metrocop is still an evolving project. There’s always another detail I want to revisit.

Sources & credits

The early build references mb814’s Metrocop model and textures, alongside Half-Life 2 and Half-Life: Alyx. The original baton used Lybos’s Police Baton model; the later design references Pixelitz’s HD remake. The replacement helmet in 2021 used an Etsy model; its creator is not identified in the original thread. These sources are distinct from my fabrication, adaptations, and later component designs.

Read the original RPF thread for the complete discussion and community contributions. Half-Life and its characters belong to Valve; this is an independent fan project.

Stunstick

Built around a PVC pipe core with 3D-printed components, the stunstick became a good test of low-cost construction and high-finish presentation. The goal was something sturdy enough to handle, but polished enough to read as a convincing in-universe prop.

Belt Boxes

The belt boxes were reverse engineered with printability, serviceability, and lighting where appropriate in mind. I designed the lit boxes around readily available puck-light components, while the switch box stayed focused on silhouette, assembly, and practical belt mounting.

Buy the light-up belt box on Etsy

Radio Box

The radio box was a good example of how I approached the whole belt-box set: reverse engineer the outer shape, make smart decisions about printability, and leave room for the lighting and battery access to work without ruining the silhouette.

1 / 8

Pager Box

The pager box leaned more on subtle form changes than surface detail, so the challenge was making sure the paneling, clip area, and front recesses felt deliberate instead of plain. Once the lighting went in, it started to feel properly alive.

1 / 9

Switch Box

The switch box was the blockiest of the three, but that did not make it simpler. It still needed the same discipline in proportions, seams, vent spacing, and mounting logic so it could sit on the belt without reading like a generic box.
